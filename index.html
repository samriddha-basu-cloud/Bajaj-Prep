Demand planning

Demand planning turns history, market signals and commercial plans into one agreed forecast per SKU, channel and region, which supply then converts into production and purchase plans. In a high-SKU consumer business it is the bridge between commercial ambition and manufacturing reality: show that you treat forecasting as a working capital and service-level lever, not a maths exercise.

Core definitions

Term Meaning FMCG example

Promotional uplift Extra demand caused by a scheme, discount or event Combo offer on e-com during the Diwali sale

Metrics and formulas

MAPE      = (1/n) × Σ |A − F| / A × 100
WMAPE     = Σ |A − F| / Σ A
Bias      = Σ (F − A) / Σ A
Accuracy  = 1 − WMAPE

Worked example (four SKUs, units in thousands)

SKU Actual Forecast Absolute error Error vs actual
A 100 90 10 10.0%
B 200 230 30 15.0%
C 50 40 10 20.0%
D 150 160 10 6.7%
Total 500 520 60 MAPE 12.9%

WMAPE = 60 / 500 = 12%, so accuracy is 88%. Bias = (520 − 500) / 500 = +4%, a mild over-forecast. Say why you prefer WMAPE: plain MAPE lets a small SKU with a big percentage error distort the average, while WMAPE weights by volume. You can try your own numbers in the Tools section.

Choosing a forecasting approach

  • Stable, seasonal items: Holt-Winters or seasonal exponential smoothing.
  • Items driven by price, promotion or weather: regression or ML with those drivers as inputs.
  • New items: analogue (like-for-like) forecasting with launch curves, then correct weekly using early sell-out.
  • Lumpy, slow items: Croston-type methods or simple safety-stock rules rather than a heavy model.
  • Always: forecast at the level where signals are cleanest, measure at the level where decisions are made (SKU-channel-depot, 1 to 3 months ahead).

Scenario questions and model answers

Q1. A key SKU's forecast accuracy in MT fell from 80% to 65% in two months. What do you do?

First split the error: is it bias (consistently high or low) or noise? Then check the usual suspects in order: unplanned promotions or listings not communicated, stock-outs that suppressed actual demand (use sell-out or order-line data, not just shipments), a price change, a data issue such as a misposted order, and a competitor move. Fix the cause, not the number: for example, add a promotion calendar feed from Sales, clean the history of stock-out periods, and re-run FVA to see whether the sales override is helping. Report the recovery target and date.

Q2. Sales gives a forecast 20% above the statistical forecast every quarter and misses it. How do you handle it?

Show the bias with data: three quarters of Sales overrides, their FVA, and the working capital or stock-out cost. Then change the process rather than argue: require a reason code and customer or scheme evidence for any override above a threshold, track override accuracy per salesperson or region, and review it in the monthly demand meeting. Agree that overrides which keep adding value stay, and those that do not are retired.

Q3. We are launching a 650 ml pack for e-commerce and MT with no history. How do you forecast it?

Use analogues: the closest existing pack or the earlier launch of a similar size, in the same channel, with its first 8 to 12 week curve. Build the forecast from channel drivers: number of listings or stores, expected rate of sale per listing per week, and planned visibility spend. Decide how much cannibalises the existing size. Start with a conservative first lot and a fast replenishment loop, review weekly against actual sell-out, and re-base after 4 to 6 weeks.

Q4. Festive e-commerce sale events are running this month. How do you plan for them?

Build the event as a separate promotional layer on top of baseline: use last year's event uplift by platform and pack, adjust for this year's deal depth and ad spend, and confirm with the e-com team. Agree inventory positions at each platform warehouse before the event, plan the post-event dip (pull-forward) so you do not over-replenish afterwards, and keep a daily review during the event with a pre-agreed rule for emergency replenishment.

Q5. Coconut oil costs rise sharply and the company takes a price increase. How does this change your forecast?

Apply price elasticity by channel: GT and rural consumers tend to respond with smaller pack down-trading, MT and e-com with promotion shifts. Forecast volume and mix separately, pull forward some sell-in ahead of the price change but net that out in the following months, and share the scenario (base, high, low elasticity) with Supply and Finance so purchase and production plans carry a range, not one number.

Q6. Quick commerce is growing much faster than the rest of e-com. What changes in your approach?

Forecast at city or dark-store cluster level with weekly buckets, use sell-out feeds for demand sensing, and keep a smaller, faster replenishment cycle than GT. Track fill rate and on-shelf availability there, because lost sales on a quick-commerce app hurt ranking and visibility as well as revenue.

Q7. Aggregate accuracy looks fine but depots keep running out of stock. Why?

Accuracy at national or brand level hides errors at SKU-depot level, which is where stock is held. Measure and manage accuracy at the decision level, look at bias by depot (some over, some under cancel each other out nationally), and rebalance with stock transfers and a better regional split of the forecast.

Scenario A. The seasonal whiplash and unsold inventory (appliances)

"You are the Demand Planner for Bajaj's seasonal appliances division (air coolers and fans). Due to unseasonal rains in April and May, secondary sales drop 35% below the S&OP consensus plan. Depots are full, working capital is choked, and factories have raw materials committed for June. Walk me through your immediate 30-day action plan."

How to structure your answer

  1. Immediate triage (stop the bleeding). Freeze production schedules for the affected SKUs that sit beyond the frozen 7-day plant horizon. Coordinate with Procurement to defer RM call-offs (motors, plastic resins) or divert common components (universal motors, packaging cartons) to non-seasonal categories such as mixer grinders or exhaust fans.
  2. Spatial re-balancing (inter-depot stock transfer). Run an inventory health check across climatic zones. While North and Central India may have unseasonal rain, South or West hubs may still have baseline demand or drier weather. Execute a Stock Transfer Order (STO) only if the holding cost plus markdown risk exceeds the secondary freight cost.
  3. Channel liquidation lever. Partner with Trade Marketing to push bundled promotions on e-commerce (upcoming Amazon or Flipkart sale events) and modern trade (Reliance Digital, Croma), where price elasticity is faster than in General Trade.
  4. Root cause and model adjustment. Re-calibrate the statistical baseline for June using short-term exponential smoothing (alpha weighted toward recent weeks) so the ERP does not auto-generate replenishment orders from last year's seasonal indices.
Scenario B. Sales vs supply conflict in S&OP (appliances)

"During the pre-Diwali S&OP meeting, the Regional Sales Manager insists on a 50% forecast uplift for kitchen appliances (OTGs, air fryers, mixer grinders). However, historical data shows their team over-forecast by 28% last Diwali, leaving you with 4 months of SLOB inventory. How do you handle this without causing a stockout if they are actually right?"

How to structure your answer

  1. Deconstruct the forecast into building blocks. Do not argue over the aggregate number. Break the 50% uplift into baseline run-rate, distribution expansion (new stores or dealers), promotional lift and new product introductions (NPI). Ask Sales to tie every incremental unit to a specific customer commitment or confirmed PO in MT or e-com.
  2. Apply Forecast Value Added (FVA) analysis. Show historical data proving where manual sales overrides degraded accuracy compared with the statistical baseline.
  3. Risk-hedged inventory postponement. Commit 100% of the statistical baseline plus confirmed MT and e-com order book as finished goods at regional C&F (carrying and forwarding) depots. For the disputed incremental 20 to 25% uplift, hold the buffer upstream at the mother warehouse (central hub) or in semi-finished or WIP form (for example unpainted sub-assemblies) with a rapid 48-hour dispatch trigger once early Navratri sell-through data validates the spike.

How to structure any demand-planning answer: diagnose, quantify, act, prevent. Only mention tools you have actually used (SAP IBP or APO, o9, Kinaxis, Excel and so on).

Inventory planning

Inventory planning sets how much stock of each item to hold, and where, so that service targets are met at the lowest tied-up cash. Show that you balance three things at once: availability, working capital, and shelf-life or obsolescence risk.

Core definitions

Term Meaning Example
Cycle stock Stock needed to cover normal demand between replenishments Depot orders weekly, so holds about half a week on average
Safety stock (SS) Buffer against demand and lead-time variability 396 units in the worked example below
Reorder point (ROP) Stock level that triggers a new order Order when stock falls to 3,996 units
Pipeline stock Stock in transit or on order Goods on a truck from plant to depot
Lead time Order-to-receipt time, including its variability Supplier takes 9 days, sometimes 12
Service level (cycle) Probability of not stocking out in a replenishment cycle 95%
Fill rate Share of ordered quantity shipped from stock 97 of 100 cases shipped = 97%
OTIF Delivered on time and in full Key MT and e-com retailer metric; fines apply on misses
Days of inventory on hand (DOH or DIO) Inventory value divided by daily cost of goods sold 49 days
Inventory turns Annual COGS divided by average inventory 7.5 turns
MOQ Minimum order quantity a supplier or plant will accept Bottle supplier's MOQ is 50,000 pieces
FEFO / FIFO First-expired-first-out / first-in-first-out dispatch FEFO for shelf-life-limited products
SLOB Slow-moving and obsolete stock Old-pack or discontinued SKUs; provisioned in accounts
DRP Distribution requirements planning: plans stock at each depot from forecast and supply lead times Computes how much the plant must send to each depot
Min-max Simple policy: reorder up to max when stock hits min Used for low-value C items
Postponement Delay final differentiation (pack or label) to reduce stock risk Hold bulk oil, fill channel-specific packs late

Formulas

SS (demand variability only)   = Z × σd × √L
SS (demand + lead-time)        = Z × √( L × σd² + d² × σL² )
ROP                            = d × L + SS
EOQ                            = √( 2 × D × S / H )
DOH (days of inventory)        = Inventory value / COGS × 365
Turns                          = COGS / Average inventory

d = average daily demand, σd = its standard deviation, L = lead time in days, σL = its standard deviation, Z = service-level factor (about 1.28 for 90%, 1.65 for 95%, 2.33 for 99%). In EOQ: D = annual demand, S = cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year.

Worked examples

  • Safety stock and ROP: d = 400 units/day, σd = 80, L = 9 days, 95% service. SS = 1.65 × 80 × 3 = 396 units. ROP = 400 × 9 + 396 = 3,996 units.
  • EOQ: D = 120,000 units/year, S = ₹2,000 per order, H = ₹6 per unit per year. EOQ = √(2 × 120,000 × 2,000 / 6) = about 8,944 units per order.
  • DOH and turns: inventory ₹12 crore, annual COGS ₹90 crore. DOH = 12 / 90 × 365 = about 49 days; turns = 90 / 12 = 7.5.
  • Cost of service: moving from 95% to 99% raises Z from 1.65 to 2.33, so safety stock rises by about 41% (2.33 / 1.65). That is why you set service targets by item class, not one number for all.

The Tools section has a safety-stock simulator so you can see how lead-time and demand variability change these numbers.

Scenario questions and model answers

Q1. Finance wants inventory down 15% without hurting service. How do you approach it?

Segment first (ABC-XYZ) and look at where the cash sits: normally a small number of slow C items and excess SLOB hold a lot of money. Then work the levers in order of safety: clear SLOB and stop re-ordering it; cut safety stock where it is oversized relative to variability or where the supplier lead time has improved; reduce batch sizes and review cycles where EOQ or MOQ is overstated; improve forecast accuracy so less buffer is needed; and rebalance across depots. Protect A items. Track DOH and fill rate together, weekly, so any service damage shows early.

Q2. Some GT depots have stock-outs while others have excess of the same SKU. What do you do?

This is an allocation and forecast-split problem, not a total-stock problem. Check depot-level forecast bias, then run lateral transfers for the immediate fix (freight cost versus lost sales), re-split future supply by actual depot sell-out, and set depot-specific safety stock by their own variability and replenishment lead time. Review monthly so the split follows demand, not the earlier plan.

Q3. A supplier's lead time moves from 10 to 20 days. What changes?

Safety stock scales with the square root of lead time, so it rises by about 41% (√2) for the same service level and demand variability, and pipeline stock doubles. If lead-time variability also worsened, SS rises more. Quantify the cash impact, then push on the cause with the supplier or procurement (second source, local supplier, vendor-managed stock, firm schedule) before accepting a permanently higher buffer.

Q4. We have 4 months of stock of an old pack before a pack change. What now?

Stop supply of that pack, estimate sell-through at current offtake, and agree a liquidation plan: route stock to channels where the old pack is acceptable (specific GT regions, institutional, exports, schemes), use promotions with a cost cap, and set a provision date with Finance if it will not clear before the remaining shelf life becomes a problem. For the next change, build a run-out plan in advance with a last-order date for the old material and packaging.

Q5. Raw material prices are rising. Should we buy ahead?

It is a trade-off: forward buying locks cost but adds carrying cost (interest, storage, shelf-life and quality risk) and ties up cash. Compare the expected price saving per tonne with the holding cost for the extra months, check storage and quality limits, and consider partial cover (for example 40 to 60% of three months' need) or fixed-price contracts rather than buying all stock. Decide jointly with Procurement and Finance.

Q6. Quick commerce and e-com need small, frequent replenishment while GT needs bulk. How do you set stock?

Hold channel-specific stock policies: higher review frequency, smaller lot sizes and tighter fill-rate targets for e-com and quick commerce, with stock positioned close to marketplace warehouses or city hubs; larger, less frequent replenishment to GT distributors. Keep a shared central buffer of upstream stock (or bulk oil, if fill is postponed) so that a surge in one channel can be served without separate large stocks everywhere.

Q7. How do you decide whether to delist a slow SKU?

Look at its contribution margin, share of working capital, forecast accuracy, complexity cost (changeovers, MOQ stock), and whether it cannibalises or supports the range or a key customer. If the volume can transfer to another pack and the SKU holds above-average inventory days, propose delisting with Sales and Marketing agreement, clear stock, and monitor volume transfer for two cycles.

Distribution

Distribution gets finished goods from plants to depots, distributors and customers on time, at the lowest cost, for three very different channels. It is a battle of channel economics and service-level differentiation: serving a local electrical or hardware shop (GT) needs a different model from serving a Blinkit dark store (quick commerce) or a D-Mart central distribution centre (MT). The job is to optimise cost per case against service level, not cost alone.

Metrics and formulas

OTIF            = Orders on time and in full / Total orders
Logistics cost% = (Freight + warehousing + handling + admin) / Net sales
Cost per case   = Total logistics cost / Cases shipped

Also track: fill rate, order-to-delivery lead time, vehicle fill (load as a percentage of capacity), damage and expiry claims as a percentage of sales, distributor stock days, and distributor ROI (distributors leave if their return is poor).

Worked example: FTL or LTL? An FTL costs ₹36,000 and carries 600 cases, so ₹60 per case at full load but ₹80 per case at 75% fill (450 cases). LTL costs ₹75 per case. Break-even = 36,000 / 75 = 480 cases, or 80% fill. Below 80% fill, LTL or consolidating with another lane is cheaper; above it, FTL wins. The lesson: pool orders to reach the break-even before dispatching.

Logistics levers

  • Primary logistics: movement from the manufacturing plant or mother hub to regional depots (C&F). Typically FTL (full truck load) or containerised rail and road, focused on volumetric weight utilisation (cube out vs weigh out), which is crucial for bulky, low-density items such as fans and air coolers versus dense items such as hair oil bottles or irons.
  • Secondary logistics: movement from the regional depot to distributors, MT DCs or e-com fulfilment centres. Often LTL (less than truck load) or milk-run routing with light commercial vehicles, where route optimisation and drop-size consolidation set the cost per unit.
  • TOC (Theory of Constraints) replenishment, or pull distribution: moving away from pushing stock to GT distributors against primary sales targets, and replenishing distributor inventory daily or weekly based on actual secondary depletion (DMS data) so distributor working capital turns fast.
  • DRP (distribution requirements planning): time-phased planning that calculates exactly when and how much stock to ship from central plants to regional depots, based on safety stock thresholds, transit lead times and projected depot-level demand.

Scenario questions and model answers

Q1. A modern-trade chain's OTIF is 85% against a 95% requirement, and penalties are rising. What do you do?

Break failures into late, short and rejected (barcode, label, documentation, expiry). For shorts, check forecast and stock at the supplying depot; for lates, look at lane transit and dispatch cut-offs; for rejections, fix paperwork and packaging at source. Agree a joint improvement plan with the chain, assign one owner per cause, hold a weekly review, and compare the cost of the penalties with the cost of holding extra stock for that customer. Target the cause with the most lines first.

Q2. A GT distributor orders heavily at month-end but secondary sales are flat. Why does it matter and what do you do?

Primary sales are running ahead of secondary sales, so distributor stock is building (channel stuffing), which later leads to returns, expiry and discounting. Check stock days in the DMS, compare primary and secondary trends, and set maximum stock-day norms. Link incentives to secondary sales, not to month-end primary, and escalate high stock-day distributors for corrective plans.

Q3. You are asked to cut logistics cost by 8% without hurting service. Where do you look?

Segment costs by lane and channel. Typical levers: raise truck fill and use pooled loading; move from many small direct shipments to hub-and-spoke where volumes are small; renegotiate or re-tender freight on high-volume lanes; rationalise depots where the lead-time impact is small; shift mode (rail or coastal) on long, non-urgent lanes; and cut damage claims. Keep service metrics visible while testing each lever on a pilot lane before scaling.

Q4. Quick-commerce sales are growing fast and the app shows our items out of stock in some cities. What now?

Availability on these apps directly affects ranking and sales, so treat it as a priority. Map stock-outs by city and warehouse, agree faster replenishment cycles with the platform (smaller and more frequent), position stock in city hubs near the platform warehouses, and set a minimum days-of-cover target per dark-store cluster. Review weekly with the e-com team and the platform's category manager.

Q5. Hair oil bottles are leaking in e-commerce and the platform is raising damage claims. What do you do?

Find where the damage happens: at our packing, in transit or at the platform warehouse, using photos and batch data. Then fix at the right source: seal and cap quality with the supplier, better secondary packaging or fillers for e-com shippers, drop-test the pack, and reduce handling steps. Agree a claims process with the platform and track damage as a percentage of sales by SKU until it falls below target.

Q6. Floods close the main road to a key state for a week. How do you maintain supply?

Activate the contingency plan: reroute via alternative lanes or depots, release safety stock from the nearest depot, prioritise high-velocity SKUs and key customers on the available trucks, communicate honestly with distributors and key accounts about revised dates, and pre-position stock ahead of known risk seasons (monsoon). Afterwards, review the plan and add alternative transporters and lanes.

Q7. We want to expand direct coverage in a new state, as the company has done in UP and MP. How do you assess it?

Compare the economics of direct reach with the distributor model: outlet universe, expected numeric distribution, cost to serve per outlet, sales team and vehicle cost, working capital in stock and credit, and the service improvement expected. Pilot in a few districts, measure incremental sales and cost per outlet, and scale only where the payback is acceptable. Plan the transition of existing distributors carefully so supply is not disrupted.

Q8. Festive e-com events are on. What do you do in distribution?

Confirm inventory targets at each platform warehouse, book inbound appointment slots early, pack to the platform's specifications, add extra dispatch shifts, monitor stock cover daily during the event with a rule for emergency replenishment, then rebalance stock after the event so no warehouse is left overstocked.

Scenario A. The channel allocation conflict: GT vs MT vs e-com

"You have 10,000 units of a high-margin, fast-moving SKU (for example a 15 L storage water heater or a flagship Almond Drops pack) at your Western Region depot. Open demand across channels is 15,000 units: 6,000 for GT distributors, 5,000 for Reliance Digital or D-Mart (MT), and 4,000 for an Amazon mega sale starting in 48 hours. How do you allocate the 10,000 units?"

How to structure your answer

  1. Reject pro-rata allocation. Cutting everyone by 33% uniformly is the worst supply chain decision: it guarantees MT penalty fines, ruins e-com event visibility and frustrates top GT dealers.
  2. Evaluate the financial and strategic cost of a stockout, channel by channel.
    • MT check: read the SLA penalty clauses and appointment lock-ins. If MT charges a 5 to 10% penalty on unfulfilled PO value and delists the SKU, prioritise the minimum threshold required to avoid penalty (if the contract allows a 90% case fill rate without penalty, allocate 4,500 units).
    • E-com check: during a flagship sale, running out of stock drops the product's organic search rank for weeks. Allocate enough stock (for example 2,500 to 3,000 units) to cover the first 48 to 72 hours of peak velocity while expediting the next batch from the plant.
    • GT check: segment GT distributors into Tier-1 (cash-and-carry, zero overdue credit, high secondary sell-out velocity via DMS) versus speculative orders. Allocate the remaining 2,500 to 3,000 units strictly to Tier-1 distributors with low physical stock cover, and fulfil the rest in the replenishment wave arriving in 3 days.
Scenario B. Optimising freight for high-volume, low-density products

"Freight cost as a percentage of revenue has spiked by 1.8% in the last quarter because trucks carrying air coolers and fans are 'cubing out' (running out of volume space) at only 55% of their rated weight capacity. How do you solve this?"

How to structure your answer

  1. Load mix optimisation (co-load dense and bulky SKUs). Implement a linear programming or load-building rule in the TMS (transportation management system). Pair low-density, high-cube products (air coolers, fans) on the top tiers with high-density, heavy products (cast-iron dry irons, water heater tanks, mixer grinders, or cartons of hair oil) at the base of the truck bed. This hits roughly 90% volume utilisation and 85% weight utilisation together.
  2. Vehicle type right-sizing. Shift primary lanes from standard rigid trucks to high-cube containerised vehicles (32 ft MXL high-cube containers) to lower the per-unit freight cost on bulky lanes.
  3. Packaging and knock-down design. Work with R&D and Packaging to see whether sub-assemblies (fan blades packed flat separately from motor units, or nestable cooler bodies) can raise carton density per cubic metre.

One line to remember: distribution is a three-way balance of service (availability and OTIF), cost (cost per case and logistics as % of sales) and cash (distributor and company stock). Name which one you are trading off in every answer.

Procurement (RM strategic sourcing)

Strategic sourcing buys raw materials at the lowest total cost for the quality and lead time the plant needs, while building suppliers who keep supply continuous. Whether Bajaj is buying copper winding wire, CRNGO electrical steel, aluminium ingots, ABS and PP engineering plastics and PCBs (Electricals) or light liquid paraffin (LLP), refined almond or vegetable oil, glass or PET bottles and HDPE caps (Consumer Care), RM typically represents 60 to 70% of COGS, so procurement is a direct P&L driver. The role is judged on cost, quality, delivery reliability and supply risk.

Scenario questions and model answers

Q1. Coconut oil or copra prices have risen 25% in three months. What is your strategy?

First understand the driver: crop size, seasonality, export demand or speculation, and how long it is likely to last. Then use a layered approach: cover a share of the next quarter's need through forward contracts or staggered buying (not 100%, to avoid locking in at the peak), widen the supplier base and regions, ask for index-linked pricing with a cap and floor, and work with Sales and Finance on pack-size, mix or price actions if the cost increase is lasting. Track landed cost versus budget weekly and brief leadership on the range of outcomes.

Q2. Your only supplier of a key fragrance has a quality failure and cannot deliver for three weeks. What do you do?

Immediate: quantify stock on hand and cover days, hold or release stock by priority SKU, and escalate with the supplier for a root cause and recovery date. Short term: start an emergency qualification of an alternative source with technical and quality teams. Longer term: move the item from single to dual sourcing with a 70:30 or 80:20 split, add buffer stock for high-risk bottleneck items, and put quality and delivery penalties into the contract.

Q3. How do you negotiate a 5% price reduction from an existing supplier?

Prepare first: should-cost model and spend history, benchmark prices, the supplier's cost drivers, your BATNA (an alternative supplier), and what you can offer in return (more volume, a longer contract, faster payment, simpler specifications). Open with facts, link the discussion to total cost instead of only price, trade concessions (volume for price, payment terms for price), and close with a written agreement and review dates. Keep the relationship intact: the aim is a better deal, not a one-time win.

Q4. A new supplier quotes 12% lower but has no track record with us. Do you switch?

Not on price alone. Audit capacity, quality systems and financial health, check references, compare total cost, and run a trial on a limited share. Start with 10 to 20% of volume, measure quality (PPM, rejections), delivery and responsiveness for a few lots, and scale only when it meets the scorecard. Keep the incumbent as a fallback until the new supplier proves reliable.

Q5. Packaging resin prices rose and your supplier asks for a 9% increase. How do you respond?

Ask for the cost build-up and compare it with the polymer index and conversion cost: only the justified part of the increase should pass through. Propose an index-linked formula with a monthly or quarterly reset in both directions, check alternative quotes, and look for offsets such as lighter bottles, standard neck finishes and larger volume commitments. Escalate only if the cost data does not support the number.

Q6. You are given a 5% annual savings target. Where do you look?

Start from spend analysis: the top categories and top suppliers. Levers: competitive re-tender of leverage items, volume consolidation, specification changes (lighter bottles, fewer variants), supplier development and yield improvement, better payment terms, alternative materials or local sourcing, and eliminating demand (specification creep). Separate true cost reduction from cost avoidance and agree with Finance how savings are counted, so the number stands up.

Q7. A supplier is late and may stop the production line tomorrow. What do you do?

Check exact stock and consumption to know the real deadline. Expedite with the supplier (air or dedicated truck, partial shipment), look for a quick alternative supplier or inter-plant transfer, and ask Production Planning to re-sequence the schedule to run items that do not need that material. Afterwards, find the root cause and fix it: safety stock, a reliable lead time, or a second supplier.

Q8. How do you build a stronger supplier relationship?

Share forecasts, give clear specifications, pay on time, hold joint quarterly reviews on quality, delivery and cost, set shared improvement goals, and reward good performance with more volume. Treat strategic suppliers as partners and non-critical ones transactionally.

Q9. The MOQ on a packaging item is far above your monthly need. What do you do?

Calculate the cost of excess stock (cash and shelf-life or obsolescence risk) against any price benefit. Negotiate a lower MOQ, pooled orders across SKUs, staggered call-offs against a bulk order, or consignment or VMI. If the item is common to several SKUs, standardise the specification so volume combines.

Q10. How would you source coconut or other agri materials responsibly?

Build a traceable base across several regions and trusted aggregators or farmer groups, set quality specifications (moisture, free fatty acid, adulteration checks), verify with lab tests at receipt, and avoid dependence on a single region or trader. Consider longer-term arrangements with farmer organisations where possible. Responsible sourcing also supports supply continuity.

Scenario A. Handling a monopoly or single-source vendor price hike

"A critical Tier-1 supplier of copper motor windings (or specialised glass bottles) demands an immediate 12% price increase citing rising energy and raw material costs. They hold 80% of your share of business (SOB) because of tooling lock-in, and your production line has only 5 days of cover. How do you respond?"

How to structure your answer

  1. Short-term continuity (protect the line). Never risk a plant shutdown over a negotiation standoff when cover is only 5 days. Separate immediate supply continuity from commercial settlement. Agree to a provisional interim dispatch while requiring an open-book cost breakdown within 72 hours.
  2. Fact-based should-cost dissection. Demand the split of the 12% claim. If raw material (copper LME) is 65% of the component cost and rose by 10%, the effect on the finished part is only 0.65 × 10% = 6.5%, not 12%. Challenge the remaining 5.5% conversion and energy claim using state industrial electricity tariff data and scrap recovery credits.
  3. Commercial trade-offs (value levers). If a partial increase is justified by index movement, offset the working-capital impact by negotiating extended payment terms (for example 45 days to 75 days), or by Vendor Managed Inventory or consignment stock where Bajaj pays only on consumption.
  4. Medium-term de-risking (dual sourcing). Start parallel tooling development with a secondary supplier to move the SOB split from 80:20 to 60:30:10, converting a strategic or bottleneck vulnerability into a leverage category over the next two quarters.
Scenario B. Balancing cost, quality and lead time in vendor selection

"You are running an RFQ for injection-moulded plastic housings for a new range of mixer grinders. Vendor A is 7% cheaper but is 1,200 km away with a 7-day lead time and a historical 2.5% defect rate. Vendor B is 7% costlier, 40 km from your assembly plant, offers JIT daily deliveries and has a 0.2% defect rate. How do you justify selecting Vendor B (or splitting the business) to a Finance team that only looks at purchase price variance (PPV)?"

How to structure your answer

  1. Translate operations metrics into finance language (TCO model). Finance cares about PPV because it is visible on the PO, whereas supply-chain friction costs sit in overheads. Build a one-page TCO comparison:
    • Inventory carrying cost: Vendor A's 7-day transit and its variability need about 10 to 12 days of extra safety stock. At a 12 to 14% cost of capital plus warehouse space for bulky plastic housings, calculate the exact rupee cost per unit.
    • Cost of poor quality (COPQ): a 2.5% defect rate on an assembly line causes line stoppages, sorting labour and reverse-logistics freight over 1,200 km.
    • Freight and damage: bulky plastic parts incur high volumetric freight over 1,200 km.
  2. The strategic split. Instead of an all-or-nothing choice, propose a dual-sourcing base-and-surge model, or use Vendor A's quote as a should-cost benchmark to negotiate Vendor B down by 3 to 4% in exchange for a long-term volume commitment and milk-run returnable packaging bins that remove corrugated carton costs.

What to emphasise: use the words total cost, supply continuity, dual sourcing, supplier development and risk. If asked about commodity hedging, say you would use forward contracts and index-linked pricing where there is no liquid futures market, and align the policy with Finance.

S&OP, working capital and KPIs

Planning, distribution and procurement only work when one monthly process keeps Sales, Supply, Procurement and Finance on the same numbers. Expect at least one question on how you resolve conflicts between these functions, whichever role you interview for.

S&OP (sales and operations planning) in five steps

  1. Data and demand review: clean actuals, statistical forecast, sales and marketing inputs, promotion and launch plans, giving one unconstrained demand plan.
  2. Supply review: plant capacity, material availability, inventory targets and logistics constraints tested against that demand.
  3. Pre-S&OP: reconcile gaps and build options (overtime, outsourcing, allocation, pricing action) with costs.
  4. Executive S&OP: leadership picks an option and locks the plan; Finance reconciles it with the budget.
  5. Execute and measure: track forecast accuracy, service, inventory and plan adherence, then feed lessons into the next cycle.

Working capital link

CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO

Example: DIO 49 days (inventory), DSO 25 days (customer credit), DPO 45 days (supplier credit) gives a cash conversion cycle of 49 + 25 − 45 = 29 days. Planning cuts DIO, distribution manages DSO (distributor and key-account credit, claims), and procurement lifts DPO. Every day saved frees cash equal to one day of sales or cost.

Scenario questions and model answers

Q1. Sales wants 98% availability on every SKU and Finance wants 30% lower inventory. How do you resolve it?

Show the cost of service with numbers (the safety-stock jump from 95 to 99% is about 41%), then segment: protect high-value, fast-moving items with high service targets, and relax slow C items. Put the trade-off to the S&OP meeting as two or three scenarios with service and cash for each, and let leadership choose. Document the decision so the targets are not re-argued each month.

Q2. Plant capacity is short in peak season. How do you allocate supply across GT, MT and e-com?

Agree allocation rules in advance: protect contractual commitments and penalties first (MT and key accounts), then contribution margin and strategic priorities (growth channels, launches), then fair share for GT based on its history. Where possible add capacity (overtime, outsourcing) or pull production forward, and communicate shortfalls early. Review at the S&OP meeting with Sales so allocation is a business decision, not a supply one.

Q3. GT is declining while e-commerce and quick commerce grow fast. What changes in planning?

Move forecasting to channel level with different drivers, review pack architecture and stock policies per channel, plan faster replenishment for e-com and quick commerce, and reassess GT distribution: which regions and outlets are worth serving at what cost. Monitor the margin mix too, because online channels often carry higher trade and platform costs even when volume rises.

Q4. The company opens direct distribution in new areas. What is the impact on planning, distribution and procurement?

Planning: new depots mean new forecast and safety-stock locations, so a more granular demand split. Distribution: new lanes, depot capacity and a transition from distributor stock to company stock. Procurement: usually no immediate change, but volume growth may justify renegotiating contracts. Finance: working capital rises at first, so set stock and credit norms early.

Q5. You inherit poor data quality: master data errors, unclean history. Where do you start?

Start with the 20% of items that make 80% of volume. Fix master data (units of measure, lead times, pack conversions), clean history of stock-out and one-off periods, and set a monthly data-quality checklist with an owner. Better data raises accuracy faster than a more complex model.

Checklist

60-second introduction (fill the brackets with your facts)

I am [name], a [role] with [X years] in [FMCG or industry], most recently at [company] where I was responsible for [scope: categories, channels, regions, value]. Two results I am proud of: [achievement 1 with number, for example lifted forecast accuracy from 68% to 80% on top SKUs] and [achievement 2 with number, for example cut inventory days by 9 without lowering fill rate]. I am excited by Bajaj because it competes across GT, modern trade and e-commerce with strong brands, and the role lets me apply [your strongest skill] to keep availability high while managing cost and cash. I enjoy working across Sales, Supply and Finance and would like to contribute to [one concrete goal].

Three habits that win points

  1. Structure: open with "I would approach this in three steps", then name them.
  2. Numbers: give a metric, a target or a range with every answer.
  3. Trade-off: state what you are giving up (service, cost or cash) and why that is the right call.
